Compactness

A subset \(\mathbb{K} \subset \mathbb{X}\) of a metric space \((\mathbb{X}, d)\) is compact if every open cover of \(\mathbb{K}\) has a finite subcover.

An open cover is any collection of open sets \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) such that
[ {A} U, ] meaning that each point of \(\mathbb{K}\) lies inside at least one of the \(U_\alpha\). A subcover is a smaller collection of these sets that still covers \(\mathbb{K}\).

Compactness says “whenever you try to cover \(\mathbb{K}\) with (possibly infinitely many) open sets, a finite selection will do the job.” Personally, I find this one of those overly mathematical notions that I’m sure exists for good reason, but it typically suffices just to say “its compact”, and to live your life. Compactness quietly guarantees that local properties can be made global, and that limits behave well inside bounded regions.

Example: Closed and Bounded Euclidean Space

In Euclidean space, a set is compact if and only if it is both closed and bounded. Again, it’s a silly math game; normal people would just think, of course it does, just pick the interval, plus minus a little bit, make it open, job done. Alas, mathematicans demand more. Without loss of generality I’ll show this for the interval \([0,1]\).

Let \(\mathcal{U} = \{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) be an open cover of \([0,1]\). We don’t have to do more than this with this assumption, \(\mathcal{U}\) just has to exist.

Define [ S = {, x : [0,x] ,}. ] That is, \(S\) is a set of all of the points up to which we can achieve finite coverage between \([0,1]\). First, note that \(S\) is nonempty since \(0 \in S\) as you can always cover a point with an open set that bounds it. Next, note that the set is bounded above by \(1\).

Let \(s = \sup S\). Assume, for contradiction, that \(s < 1\). Because \(\mathcal{U}\) covers \([0,1]\), there exists some \(U_{\alpha_0} \in \mathcal{U}\) such that \(s \in U_{\alpha_0}\). As \(U_{\alpha_0}\) is open, there exists \(\eta > 0\) with \((s - \eta, s + \eta) \subset U_{\alpha_0}\). This means we can cover a bit beyond \(s\) using the same set \(U_{\alpha_0}\).

By the definition of \(s\) as a supremum (i.e. maximum), for any \(\eta>0\) there exists \(x\in S\) with \(s-\eta/2<x\le s\). Intuitively, \(x\) is a point just short of \(s\) that we can already reach with finitely many sets. Since \(x\in S\), the segment \([0,x]\) can be covered by finitely many members of \(\mathcal{U}\); denote this finite subcover by \(\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{U}\). Because \(s\) lies in the open set \(U_{\alpha_0}\), there exists \(\eta>0\) such that \((s-\eta,s+\eta)\subset U_{\alpha_0}\), so this same open set covers a little interval past \(s\). Hence [ [0,{1,s+/2}]{[0,x] U_{_0}}. ] The right-hand side is the union of finitely many sets from \(\mathcal{U}\) (those in \(\mathcal{V}\) together with \(U_{\alpha_0}\)) so \([0,\min\{1,s+\eta/2\}]\) is finitely covered. This contradicts the definition of \(s\) as the least upper bound of \(S\), because it shows that a point larger than \(s\) can also be finitely covered. Therefore \(s=1\).

Use Case: Lebesgue Number Theorem

One useful place I find myself envoking compactness is justifying the existence of a finite rectangular cover. This can be justified by the Lebesgue Number Theorem. The Theorem states:

Let \((\mathbb{X}, d)\) be a compact metric space and let \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) be an open cover of \(\mathbb{X}\). Then there exists a number \(\lambda > 0\) (called a Lebesgue number for the cover) such that for every subset \(S \subset \mathbb{X}\) with \(\mathrm{diam}(S) < \lambda\),
[ S U_ A. ]

In particular, every ball \(B(x, \lambda)\) of radius \(\lambda\) around any point \(x \in \mathbb{X}\) is contained in at least one member of the cover.

Corollary (Finite Rectangular Cover).
Let \(I \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) be a compact rectangle and \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) an open cover of \(I\). Then there exists \(\lambda > 0\) such that any closed rectangle \(R \subset I\) with side lengths less than \(\lambda / \sqrt{n}\) satisfies
[ R U_ . ] Consequently, \(I\) can be partitioned into finitely many such rectangles \(\{R_j\}\), each contained in some \(U_\alpha\).

Compactness ensures the existence of a global “small enough” scale \(\lambda\) that works uniformly across the space. Once \(\lambda\) is known, we can subdivide a compact region into finitely many smaller boxes of diameter less than \(\lambda\), knowing that each box will fit entirely inside one of the open sets of the cover.
