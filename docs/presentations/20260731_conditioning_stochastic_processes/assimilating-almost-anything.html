Assimilating Almost Anything into Almost Any Stochastic Model

Lachlan Astfalck

UNSW Sydney | STREAM


What are we trying to do?


  • Take a stochastic model you can run forward: a solver, a stochastic parameterisation, a field simulator. Call it a stochastic process \(f\).
  • We want runs that assimilate/agree with some information \(\mathcal{C}\):
    • sparse, indirect, bounded-below observations — gauges, satellite retrievals, floats;
    • a budget or a boundary condition the analysis has to respect;
    • an event we want to impose — a heatwave of a given severity, a surge above a given level.
  • Working assumption: running the unconditional model forward is easy, steering/conditioning is hard.


We are targeting the quantity \(p(f \mid \mathcal{C})\).

Some Nomenclature


  • Field - some general notion of a latent/unobserved/incomplete physical process
  • Assimilation - incorporating information into your field
  • Model - some structured belief on your field (not necessarily a numerical model)
  • Stochastic Model - any structured belief with some formal mechanism that may be perturbed

Noising a cat



Reversing a cat

  • Write \(p_t\) for the marginal of \(\boldsymbol{f}_t\) — the law of the process after noising for time \(t\)
    • (so \(p_0 = p_\text{cat} = \mathcal{SP}\), \(p_1 \approx \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \mathbf{I})\))
  • Anderson (1982) showed that the time reversal is also an SDE, \[\begin{aligned} \text{Forwards:}& \qquad \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_t = - \tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t) \boldsymbol{f}_t \mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{W}_t. \\ \text{Backwards:}& \qquad \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_t = \beta(t)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{f}_t - \nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t)\right]\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}_t. \end{aligned}\]
  • One unknown object: the time-dependent score \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}} \log p_t\).
  • If we know the score, noise flows backwards into cats.

Anderson (1982), Reverse-time diffusion equation models, Stochastic Processes and their Applications 12(3):313–326.

What does machine learning do?


  • For cats, \(\nabla_\boldsymbol{f} \log p_t\) is unknown. Diffusion models learn it by fitting a network \(s_\theta(\boldsymbol{f}, t)\).
  • That is the entire “ML” content of a diffusion model; everything else is stochastic calculus.
    • find a few hundred million photographs of cats;
    • noise every one of them along the forward process;
    • train \(s_\theta(\boldsymbol{f}, t) \approx \nabla_\boldsymbol{f} \log p_t\) on the pairs;
    • sample from \(p_\text{cats}\) by pushing white noise through the reverse SDE.


Conditional cats


  • Want a grumpy cat? Bayes: \[ \nabla \log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t \mid \mathcal{C} = \text{grumpy}) = \nabla \log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t) + \underbrace{\nabla \log p_t(\mathcal{C} = \text{grumpy} \mid \boldsymbol{f}_t)}_{\text{the pull towards } \mathcal{C}}. \]
  • Add a likelihood term to the drift and the trajectory bends towards draws satisfying \(\mathcal{C}\): \[\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_t = \beta(t)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{f}_t - \nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t \mid \mathcal{C})\right]\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}_t.\]
  • Machine learning calls this guidance. It is Bayes’ rule added to a drift.

Your Model Is A Boring Cat

Gaussian Processes / Kriging / OI

  • A Gaussian process is a distribution over fields: any finite set of locations has a joint normal distribution with covariance \(k(x,x')\).
  • With linear Gaussian observations \(\boldsymbol{y} = \mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{f}_0 + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), the posterior mean is closed form, \[ \mathbf{m}_{*|\boldsymbol{y}} = \mathbf{m}_* + \mathbf{K}_{**}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{K}_{**}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}} + \boldsymbol{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\!(\boldsymbol{y} - \mathbf{L}\mathbf{m}_*). \] This is optimal interpolation, with \(\mathbf{K}_{**} \equiv \mathbf{B}\).

Targeting a GP with a Diffusion

The marginal \(p_t\) stays Gaussian, and we know \[ p_t = \mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{b}(t),\, \mathbf{A}(t)\big), \qquad \boldsymbol{b}(t) = \alpha(t)\,\mathbf{m}_*, \qquad \mathbf{A}(t) = \alpha^2(t)\mathbf{K}_{**} + (1-\alpha^2(t))\mathbf{I}. \]

So the score is closed form, and no network is required:

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t) = -\mathbf{A}(t)^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol{f}_t - \boldsymbol{b}(t)\big). \]

Drop it into the reverse-time SDE and integrate from \(t=1\) to \(t=0\):

\[ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_t = \beta(t)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{f}_t - \nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{f}_t)\right]\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}_t. \]

A GP/OI is a very boring cat with a score we can write down.

Your model is already the generator


  • A draw from the process is a deterministic map of simple noise: \[ \boldsymbol{f}_0 = T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0), \qquad T_\vartheta : \mathbb{R}^{m} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\xi}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \mathbf{I}_m). \]
  • For a Gaussian field, \(T_\vartheta\) is the Cholesky factor. For an SDE or an SPDE, \(T_\vartheta\) is the integrator and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0\) are its driving increments.
  • Run the diffusion in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), where the prior is standard normal and the score is exactly \(-\boldsymbol{\xi}_t\), then push through \(T_\vartheta\).
  • Rosenblatt (1952): after discretisation, every process admits such a representation.

Rosenblatt (1952), Remarks on a multivariate transformation, The Annals of Mathematical Statistics 23(3):470–472.

A gallery of boring cats


Every one is \(\boldsymbol{f}_0 = T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0)\) for a tractable generator \(T_\vartheta\) and iid-normal latent \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0\):

  • Gaussian and heavy-tailed fields — the usual spatial priors, plus tails that match precipitation and surge: a Gaussian draw times a scale or mixing variable.
  • Categorical fields — sea ice, land cover, cloud regime: ordered labels read off latent noise (e.g. a Potts model).
  • SDEs/SPDEs — stochastic physics parameterisations, energy-balance and box models: \(T_\vartheta\) is the Euler-Maruyama step and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0\) are its increments.
  • “Stochastic” numerical models — WRF, MOM, ROMS, a Lagrangian particle tracker, …


Where we stand


  • Push \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{I})\) through \(T_\vartheta\)
  • This gives marginal samples of nearly any stochastic process. (We could already do this, it’s just simulation.)
  • The score of the latent noising process with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_t\) is closed form.
  • Thus the boring-cat property survives in latent space for every process in the gallery.

Grumpy Stochastic Models

Guidance is just Bayes


We work in latent space. Target the pulled-back posterior: \[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0 \mid \mathcal{C}) \propto p\big(\mathcal{C} \mid T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0)\big) \rho(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0). \]

Along the latent flow, the score decomposes: \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}} \log p_t(\boldsymbol{\xi}_t \mid \mathcal{C}) = \underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}} \log p_t(\boldsymbol{\xi}_t)}_{=\,-\boldsymbol{\xi}_t\ \text{(exact)}} + \underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}} \log p_t(\mathcal{C} \mid \boldsymbol{\xi}_t)}_{\text{guidance}}. \]

Run the same SDE in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\)-space, add the guidance term, then push through \(T_\vartheta\).


The latent prior is exact at every \(t\). Guidance is the only thing to approximate.

Guidance, in practice

The increment is the pull towards \(\mathcal{C}\), averaged over where the current draw could still end up: \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\log p_t(\mathcal{C}\mid \boldsymbol{\xi}_t) \approx \alpha(t) \sum_{i=1}^{S} \bar{w}^{(i)}\, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\log p\big(\mathcal{C}\mid T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0^{(i)})\big), \qquad \bar w^{(i)} \propto p\big(\mathcal{C}\mid T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0^{(i)})\big). \]

  • For us, guidance is a Monte Carlo average.
  • Each term is one gradient of the observation model, pulled back through the model: \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\log p\big(\mathcal{C}\mid T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0)\big) = \underbrace{\big[\partial_{\boldsymbol{\xi}} T_\vartheta(\boldsymbol{\xi}_0)\big]^{\!\mathsf T}}_{\text{Jacobian of the integrator}}\, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{f}_0}\log p(\mathcal{C}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_0). \]
  • No training set, no MCMC, no bespoke derivation for each new \(\mathcal{C}\).

(There are known weight-degeneracy issues in importance sampling that do not appear to matter here, for a handful of esoteric reasons.)

The model derivative


  • You need \(\partial_{\boldsymbol{\xi}} T_\vartheta\): the derivative of the integrator with respect to its own random draws. This is the same class of object 4D-Var needs, obtained by autodiff through the solver.
  • NeuralGCMs are the existence proof that a serious climate model can be written this way.
  • Unlike 4D-Var, the output is draws from the posterior, so you can compute any functional you like and honest uncertainty comes free.
  • Unlike 4D-Var and the EnKF, the observation model may be non-Gaussian, bounded, censored, or an inequality.
  • Where the code is not differentiable there are some sneaky tricks that we can do.


A Computational Caveat

  • We are not cheapening a hard computation. An expensive model is expensive to condition.
  • Cost per posterior draw \(\approx\) \(\mathcal{O}\)(reverse-SDE steps) forward model integrations.
  • Ensemble members can be generated in parallel.
  • Where a model is prohibitively expensive, we may still use an emulator, but this is a matter of practicality not feasibility.

When do neural networks still help?


  • Our thesis so far: if you can simulate, the score is free and the network goes away
  • This does nothing for computation
  • A generative emulator may still be helpful to enable computation
  • A NN provides an adjoint. An emulator is differentiable by construction; forty years of Fortran is not.
  • A learned emulator might account for model misspecification (e.g. GenCast vs ENS)

The conditioning mechanism does not care where the score comes from. Closed form from your solver, or learned from data.
