Becoming a Theory Wonk: Notes, Proofs and Pain

Convergence

At some point in mathematics, everything becomes about sequences.
We want to understand what happens as things get closer, smaller, or repeated indefinitely — and to do that, we need a way of saying “this thing settles down.”
That is convergence.

Formally, let \((\mathbb{X}, d)\) be a metric space, and let \((x_n)\) be a sequence in \(\mathbb{X}\).
We say that \((x_n)\) converges to a point \(x \in \mathbb{X}\) if, for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \[ d(x_n, x) < \epsilon \quad \text{for all } n > N. \] We write this as \(x_n \to x\), or equivalently, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = x\).

Intuitively, this just means that beyond some point, all terms of the sequence live inside any arbitrarily small \(\epsilon\)–ball around \(x\).
No magic — just a formal way of saying “eventually, and thereafter, we stay close.”

1.0.1 Example

In \(\mathbb{R}\) with the usual distance, the sequence \[ x_n = 1 + \frac{1}{n} \] converges to \(1\), because the terms can be made as close as you like to \(1\) by choosing \(n\) large enough.
Formally, given any \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(N > 1/\epsilon\); then for all \(n > N\),
\[ |x_n - 1| = \frac{1}{n} < \epsilon. \]

1.0.2 Convergent vs. Cauchy

A sequence that converges will always be Cauchy, meaning its elements eventually get arbitrarily close to one another.
Formally, \((x_n)\) is Cauchy if, for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(N\) such that for all \(m, n > N\), \[ d(x_n, x_m) < \epsilon. \] The reverse is true only in complete spaces — those where every Cauchy sequence has a limit that actually exists inside the space.
This is what makes the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\) special: they’re complete.
The rationals \(\mathbb{Q}\) are not — plenty of Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb{Q}\) “want” to converge to an irrational number that isn’t in the set.

1.0.3 Limit Points

Sometimes a sequence doesn’t converge, but parts of it do.
A point \(x \in \mathbb{X}\) is a limit point (or accumulation point) of \((x_n)\) if there exists a subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) that converges to \(x\).
Compact sets guarantee the existence of at least one limit point — this is the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem, and it’s the bridge between convergence and compactness.

1.0.4 Why This Matters

Convergence is the grammar of analysis.
Continuity, compactness, integration, and most of asymptotic statistics are just variations of this same idea: things approach, limits exist, and what happens in the limit is meaningful.
Convergence gives structure to infinity — it lets us talk about what happens “eventually” without having to get there.

Aside: A surprising amount of analysis can be understood by imagining the world as an enormous collection of sequences trying (and sometimes failing) to calm down.
